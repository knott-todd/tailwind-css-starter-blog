Written for COOP 2100 in November 2026 (lightly edited for grammar). It connects my values, my EQ results, and the course reading on ESG to the work I want to do.

**Impact on Society and the Environment:** the essay names climate change and the UN Sustainable Development Goals as the problems I want my engineering to address.

**Ethics and Equity:** the essay argues that ability carries a duty to serve, and frames the goal around the people climate change hits hardest.

**Economics and Project Management:** it uses the unit economics of electrifying industrial heat to argue that sustainability work only lasts if it is economically sustainable.

---

My mother has a saying: "Smart for what?" Her point is that keeping your gifts and abilities to yourself is selfish, and you should have a heart of service for your community. That's how I was raised, and it shapes the engineer I want to be: someone who figures out the hard problem, then brings other people along to solve it. I love understanding how things work. I've built video games and tried building a computer from transistors just to find out, and that curiosity could pull me anywhere. So I choose where it goes. Right now, that's climate change, because it could literally end the world. After that, I want to work on other UN Sustainable Development Goals, like ending hunger or getting people clean water (United Nations, 2015). The future I want my work to help create is one where our biggest concerns are all first-world problems.

In that future, a family in a low-lying coastal town no longer plans its year around the next flood, and a child spends the morning in class instead of walking to collect water. Getting there takes environmental work, like cutting emissions, and economic work, like keeping communities from losing their income to disasters and disease. This week's reading argues that companies should build environmental, social and governance (ESG) goals into how they make money, because it only lasts if it's economically sustainable (Schear et al., 2022). I've found the same thing coming at it from the outside. When I ran the unit economics on electrifying industrial heat, the technology wasn't the blocker. Electricity cost more than the gas it would replace, so plants had little reason to switch. Wanting to do good isn't enough. The numbers have to work too for it to be economically sustainable.

My top values are helping people who have needs, doing intellectually stimulating work, and getting involved in my community, and you can see them in my second year. I took Mechanics of Materials and Thermodynamics. Mechanics relied on statics, which wasn't a listed prerequisite, so much of the class was lost, me included. I taught myself statics, but a lot of my classmates were still complaining, so I started teaching them. I recorded explainer videos, set up a Discord where people could work through assignments together, and hosted live sessions, a lot of them for Thermodynamics. People really appreciated it, and that teaching became the focus of the letter that earned me the Lassonde Scholarship. Coaching and mentoring is my strongest EQ competency (Goleman & Boyatzis, 2017), and it's the "bring people along" half of the engineer I want to be.

This weekend I hosted “Shut Up, the Planet's Burning,” a climate event where I presented what the research says we can actually do about emissions. Five people came, and we're meeting Tuesday to pick our first problem to work on, possibly electrifying industrial heat. That's my answer to "Smart for what?": understand the hard problems, then get people together to solve them.
